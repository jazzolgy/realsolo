# Bill Evans Compilation — 100-Pass Analytical Study v0.1

## What “100 passes” means

This is not a claim of 100 full-duration real-time human listening runs.

The owner-supplied compilation was segmented into 24 tracks, then evaluated
through **100 independent parameterized analytical passes**:

10 analysis families × 10 parameter settings each.

The purpose is robustness: a feature that appears in one setting may be an
artifact; a feature that survives many settings becomes a better target for
repeated listening and score-aligned verification.

All results below are mixed-audio observations/navigation evidence. They are
**not isolated bass measurements and not runtime priors**.

## 100-pass families

1. low-band attack density × 10 thresholds
2. full-band attack density × 10 thresholds
3. low-band sustain/body proxy × 10 smoothing settings
4. low-band accent variability × 10 thresholds
5. low/full-band attack coupling × 10 time lags
6. low-frequency presence × 10 band-weight settings
7. dark/body timbre proxy × 10 centroid penalties
8. macro dynamic arc × 10 smoothing windows
9. role-shift/change proxy × 10 low-band weights
10. section-role contrast × 10 segmentation granularities

## Stable findings that survived parameter changes

### 1. Nardis repeatedly presents a strong low-frequency identity

Across all low-frequency-presence and dark/body parameter variants, Nardis
remains at or near the top.

This does **not** prove “the bassist plays more notes.” It instead makes Nardis
a priority case for separating:
- attack count,
- repeated/pedal identity,
- note body,
- register anchoring,
- accent,
- arrangement/mix.

Research implication:
productive repetition must not be automatically penalized as template failure.

### 2. Israel also keeps a strong low-frequency identity

Israel stays high through low-frequency-presence variants while its global
change-point score is comparatively lower.

Working question:
Can a bass line maintain strong structural presence without constantly changing
surface behavior?

This is useful for distinguishing **presence / gravity** from **variation**.

### 3. Autumn Leaves repeatedly shows strong internal change activity

Across the role-shift/change parameter sweep, Autumn Leaves remains one of the
strongest change cases.

At the same time its written harmony is highly recurrent.

This makes it a particularly useful test:
- same harmonic location,
- different chorus,
- different register / connection / articulation / density.

If verified by score-aligned listening, this supports a major RealSolo idea:
**harmonic roadmap can stay stable while realization strategy changes.**

### 4. Waltz For Debby shows high section-level contrast

Across multiple section segmentations, Waltz For Debby repeatedly scores high
for large-scale sectional contrast.

This supports studying 3/4 bass behavior by **form position**, not by one fixed
waltz template.

Compare directly with:
- Someday My Prince Will Come
- Alice In Wonderland

The key question is not merely “how to bass in 3/4,” but:
**how does the bass role change inside different 3/4 phrase/form contexts?**

### 5. Someday My Prince Will Come also has strong section-level contrast

This survives most segmentation settings.

It is therefore a useful paired study with Waltz For Debby:
same broad meter family, but potentially different density/accent/role arcs.

Do not generalize one Bill Evans waltz behavior to the other before alignment.

### 6. Blue In Green consistently shows a large macro-dynamic arc

Blue In Green remains high across the macro-dynamic smoothing sweep while its
overall attack density is not among the busiest cases.

Working interpretation to verify by ear:
**large expressive change can occur without dense note activity.**

This makes note body, silence, decay and dynamic relation high-priority features.

### 7. My Foolish Heart has low attack density but high accent variability

Across threshold changes:
- attack density remains relatively low,
- strong-event variability remains high.

This suggests a valuable listening distinction:
few attacks do not imply low expressive information.

For Bass:
attack weight, duration and release may carry more phrase information than
connective-note count.

### 8. Peri's Scope is unusually stable in dense-event behavior

Peri's Scope stays high in attack-density measures and low in accent-variability
rankings, while also producing strong local change scores.

This is a good fast-harmony test:
- continuous event flow,
- relatively consistent body,
- local structural changes.

Working question:
Does the bass simplify pitch vocabulary while maintaining directional motion
when harmonic throughput increases?

### 9. Alice In Wonderland remains valuable as an active 3/4 contrast case

Alice In Wonderland shows strong low-frequency/timbral presence in several
settings but does not behave exactly like Waltz For Debby or Someday My Prince
Will Come.

This reinforces the decision not to create a single “jazz waltz bass rule.”

### 10. Cross-band coupling varies greatly by track

Low-band attacks and full-band attacks align strongly in some tracks and weakly
in others, and the ranking changes somewhat with time lag.

This is especially important conceptually:
bass attacks need not always coincide with the strongest full-trio attacks.

Candidate hypothesis:
**independent bass phrasing and ensemble lock are both valid states; the player
must know which role it currently occupies.**

## What the 100 passes changed in the research agenda

Before this analysis it was tempting to focus on:
- more passing-tone vocabulary,
- more anti-template pressure,
- more random articulation variation.

The 100-pass result points elsewhere.

Higher-priority research questions are now:

1. **Time-floor semantics**
   - continuous walking
   - sparse anchors
   - waltz grounding
   - repeated/pedal identity
   - long harmonic gravity

2. **Note-body semantics**
   - attack
   - sounding length
   - decay
   - release timing
   - silence after the note

3. **Role-conditioned density**
   - more notes is not always more active musically
   - fewer attacks may carry greater structural weight

4. **Productive repetition**
   - identity-preserving repetition
   - mechanical scorer repetition
   must be separated.

5. **Piano–Bass complementarity**
   - bass behavior must be evaluated relative to piano density/register/activity,
     not only harmony.

6. **Form-position realization**
   - same harmony across choruses may intentionally produce different route,
     register, body and accent choices.

## Priority listening windows after 100 passes

The computational passes are used only to tell the human/score-aligned study
where to listen repeatedly.

Highest-priority comparisons:

### Autumn Leaves
Compare the recurring harmonic cycle across multiple choruses.
Focus on:
- direction,
- register,
- approach family,
- note length,
- accent,
- piano-density relation.

### Nardis
Separate:
- low-frequency presence,
- repeated identity,
- attack density,
- pedal-like behavior,
- register anchoring.

### Blue In Green / My Foolish Heart
Study sparse-information bass:
- how long notes carry harmony,
- release timing,
- silence,
- interaction with piano voicing decay.

### Waltz For Debby / Someday My Prince Will Come / Alice In Wonderland
Three-way 3/4 comparison:
- beat hierarchy,
- subdivisions,
- register motion,
- phrase-position role changes,
- independence vs lock with drums.

### Israel / Peri's Scope
Study structural marking under higher event activity:
- which changes are accented,
- which are passed through,
- where bass simplifies despite harmonic motion.

## Promotion rule

No observation in this file becomes:
- Shared Trio Grammar,
- Bill Evans LegendProfile prior,
- Bass player policy

until it survives:
1. repeated listening,
2. score/form alignment,
3. source/personnel verification when attribution matters,
4. comparison against other recordings/artists if proposed as generic grammar.

## Current conclusion

The strongest result of 100 passes is not a new lick or note-selection rule.

It is that **bass intelligence must model role, note body, time-floor semantics,
productive repetition, and form-position variation as first-class musical
decisions.**

Pitch vocabulary alone is not enough.
